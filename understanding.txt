A1. GLOBAL AND INDIAN SCALE

  India, GLOBOCAN 2022 (IARC/WHO):
    - 1,413,316 new cancer cases
    - 916,827 cancer deaths
    - Roughly 3 in 5 people diagnosed with cancer in India die of it.
      (Compare: in high-income systems, ~2 in 5.)
    - India ranks 3rd in South-East Asia for incidence, 2nd for mortality.
    - Mortality is rising FASTER in females (1.2–4.4% annual % change over the
      last decade) than males (1.2–2.4%).
    Source: Lancet Regional Health SE Asia, 2025 analysis of GLOBOCAN 2022;
            IARC India Fact Sheet.

  THE KEY INFERENCE TO STATE ON STAGE:
    The mortality-to-incidence ratio in India (~0.65) is far worse than in
    high-income countries (~0.4). That gap is NOT mainly explained by treatment
    quality. It is explained by STAGE AT PRESENTATION. This single sentence is
    the entire justification for your project.

A2. STAGE AT DIAGNOSIS — THE ACTUAL PROBLEM

  Population-based data, Trivandrum District Cancer Registry (JCO Global
  Oncology, 2019):
    - 54% of all patients presented at Stage III or IV
    - By site:  breast 37% | cervix 39% | ORAL CAVITY 67% | LUNG 88%

  National Cancer Registry Programme, pooled hospital-based registries:
    - Majority present locally advanced / locoregional:
      breast 57.0% | cervix uteri 60.0% | head & neck 66.6% | stomach 50.8%
    Source: NCRP, JCO Global Oncology "Cancer Statistics 2020".

  Cervical cancer specifically: ~70% or more are Stage III+ at diagnosis in
  India (historical Bangalore registry data).

A3. WHY STAGE IS EVERYTHING — THE "STAGE SHIFT" ARGUMENT

  Five-year survival by stage (Canadian population data; Italian data similar):
    Breast:      Stage I 100%  →  II 92%  →  III 74%  →  IV 23%
    Colon:       Stage I 96.7% →  II 83.4% → III 70.8% → IV 16.2%

  MEMORISE THIS FRAMING:
    "A woman diagnosed with Stage I breast cancer has essentially normal life
     expectancy. The same woman, same tumour biology, diagnosed at Stage IV,
     has roughly a 1-in-4 chance of being alive in five years. Nothing about
     the cancer changed. Only the calendar did."

  This is called STAGE SHIFT — moving the distribution of diagnoses from late
  stage to early stage. It is the single measurable outcome your system targets.
  Say "our target outcome metric is stage shift," not "we save lives."

A4. THE ECONOMIC AND FAMILY BURDEN (the "family distress" clause in the PS)

  - Cancer causes the HIGHEST catastrophic health expenditure (CHE) of any
    disease category requiring hospitalisation in India: 79% CHE and 43%
    distress financing.
  - Breast cancer longitudinal cohort, Tata Memorial Centre, 2019–2022 (n=500):
    CHE 84.2%, distress financing 72.4%. Worse among rural, poor and
    agriculture-dependent households.
  - Mean out-of-pocket expenditure per cancer hospitalisation: ~INR 57,232 —
    highest of any condition.
  - Insurance coverage existed for ~75% of that breast cancer cohort but
    reduced CHE incidence by only 14 percentage points.
    Sources: PLOS One 2018; Int J Equity Health 2024.

  DEFINITIONS (use them precisely):
    Catastrophic Health Expenditure (CHE) — out-of-pocket health spending
      exceeding a threshold share (commonly 10% or 40%) of household
      consumption or capacity to pay.
    Distress financing — paying for care by borrowing, selling assets, or
      taking contributions from friends/family.

  THE ARGUMENT: late-stage cancer is not just deadlier, it is
  DISPROPORTIONATELY more expensive — longer treatment, more modalities,
  palliative care, lost wages, caregiver time. Early detection is a
  poverty-prevention intervention, not just a clinical one.

PART B — THE ANATOMY OF DIAGNOSTIC DELAY
================================================================================

This part is the intellectual spine of your project. Learn it cold. It gives you
a vocabulary that instantly signals you understand the domain.

B1. THE MODEL OF PATHWAYS TO TREATMENT (Walter et al., 2012)
    and THE AARHUS STATEMENT (Weller et al., British Journal of Cancer, 2012)

  These are the internationally accepted frameworks for decomposing "delay" into
  measurable intervals. The Aarhus Statement exists specifically to standardise
  how researchers define these time points. Cite them.

  The pathway, with the standard interval names:

    [First bodily change]
        |
        |   APPRAISAL INTERVAL
        |   (person notices something; interprets it; decides whether it means
        |    anything). Ends when they decide they need to see someone.
        v
    [Decision to seek help]
        |
        |   HELP-SEEKING INTERVAL
        |   (from decision to actually attending first consultation)
        v
    [FIRST PRESENTATION to a health professional]
        |
        |   DIAGNOSTIC INTERVAL  = PRIMARY CARE INTERVAL + SECONDARY CARE
        |                          INTERVAL
        |   - Primary care interval: first presentation -> referral
        |   - Secondary care interval: referral -> diagnosis
        v
    [DIAGNOSIS]
        |
        |   PRE-TREATMENT INTERVAL
        v
    [Treatment starts]

  IMPORTANT NUANCE — "date of first presentation" is defined by Aarhus as the
  point at which it would have been AT LEAST POSSIBLE for the clinician to have
  begun investigation or referral for possible serious pathology. That precise
  definition matters, because it is the point from which the system (not the
  patient) becomes accountable.

B2. WHERE AIRA INTERVENES — MAP EVERY FEATURE TO AN INTERVAL

  This mapping is your best slide. It shows you are not building features
  randomly.

    APPRAISAL INTERVAL
      Problem: person doesn't know a 3-week ulcer is abnormal.
      AIRA:    Symptom Persistence Clock + awareness/pictogram layer.
               Converts a vague worry into a countable, visible number of days.

    HELP-SEEKING INTERVAL
      Problem: low awareness, distance, cost, fatalism, stigma, opportunity cost
               of a day's lost wage.
      AIRA:    Non-alarming, specific prompt ("go to the PHC, ask for X"),
               local language + voice, ASHA-linked outreach.

    PRIMARY CARE INTERVAL  <-- THIS IS AIRA'S UNIQUE CONTRIBUTION
      Problem: repeat presentations, symptomatic treatment, no investigation,
               information lost between visits.
      AIRA:    Diagnostic Loop Detector + Doctor Handoff Card.

    SECONDARY CARE INTERVAL
      Problem: referral made but not acted on; patient lost to follow-up.
      AIRA:    Referral tracking + ASHA/PHC dashboard follow-up queue.

    (Screening pathway runs in parallel, for the ASYMPTOMATIC — see PART D.)

B3. HARD DATA ON REPEAT CONSULTATIONS (this justifies the Loop Detector)

  English National Cancer Patient Experience / audit data:
    - Of 73,462 patients later diagnosed with cancer, 13,280 (18.1%) had seen
      their GP THREE OR MORE times before referral.
    - Among patients whose diagnosis involved at least one primary care
      consultation, 22.9% had ≥3 pre-referral consultations.
    - For RARER cancers, the proportion with ≥3 pre-referral consultations
      ranged from >30% to 60%.
    - Primary care interval scales with consultation count: roughly 1 month,
      1.5 months, and 3 months for patients with 3, 4, and ≥5 pre-referral
      consultations respectively.
    Source: BJGP 2016 (Lyratzopoulos et al. and related), NCPES analyses.

  THE ARGUMENT TO MAKE:
    "This is data from the NHS — a system with universal coverage, electronic
     records, continuity of care, and a national 2-week-wait referral standard.
     Nearly one in five cancer patients still needed three or more visits before
     anyone referred them. Now picture a system with none of those safeguards.
     That is rural India. Our Loop Detector is the safeguard that does not
     currently exist there."

  That sentence is the strongest thing you can say in the whole pitch. It turns
  a UK statistic into an argument about India.

B4. WHY THE PRIMARY CARE INTERVAL IS THE RIGHT TARGET

  Three reasons, all defensible:
   1. It is the longest modifiable interval for many cancers.
   2. It is where the information loss happens — each visit is a fresh start.
   3. It is the only interval where a cheap software intervention can act,
      because it requires no new diagnostic hardware, only memory and rules.

PART C — WHY MISDIAGNOSIS HAPPENS
================================================================================

Do not say "doctors make mistakes." Say the mechanism. Naming the cognitive
error is what makes you sound informed and non-accusatory — and it reframes
AIRA as helping clinicians rather than policing them.

C1. THE BASE-RATE PROBLEM (the honest root cause)

  Cancer is RARE in primary care. A typical full-time GP sees roughly 8–9 new
  cancers per year among thousands of consultations. Symptoms that turn out to
  be cancer are overwhelmingly caused by benign disease.

  Real positive predictive values (PPV) of classic "alarm symptoms" in primary
  care — Hamilton et al., BMJ 2007, General Practice Research Database:

    Rectal bleeding    -> colorectal cancer:  2.4% (men), 2.0% (women)
    Haemoptysis        -> respiratory cancer: 7.5% (men), 4.3% (women)
    Dysphagia          -> oesophageal cancer: 5.7% (men), 2.4% (women)

    Age-stratified spikes:
    Haemoptysis, men 75–84:  17.1%
    Dysphagia,   men 65–74:   9.0%

  READ THAT AGAIN. Rectal bleeding — the textbook red flag — is cancer about
  2 times in 100. Which means the doctor who says "it's probably piles" is
  RIGHT 98 times out of 100.

  THIS IS THE CENTRAL INSIGHT OF YOUR ENTIRE PROJECT:
    Misdiagnosis in cancer is not usually incompetence. It is a correct
    probabilistic judgement at a single point in time. The error is not the
    first "it's probably benign." The error is the SECOND and THIRD one — when
    nobody updates on the fact that the benign explanation has now failed.

    A single symptom is weak evidence. A symptom that has SURVIVED TWO FAILED
    TREATMENTS is strong evidence. Nobody currently computes that, because
    nobody holds the history.

  Say this on stage. It is respectful to clinicians, technically correct, and it
  is the exact reason a longitudinal system is the right answer.

C2. NAMED COGNITIVE FAILURE MODES

  ANCHORING — fixing on the initial impression (e.g. "acidity") and failing to
    adjust as new information arrives.
  PREMATURE CLOSURE — accepting a diagnosis before it is fully verified; the
    single most common cognitive contributor to diagnostic error.
  AVAILABILITY HEURISTIC — judging likelihood by what comes to mind easily. In
    a high-TB-burden district, a persistent cough is "TB" long before it is
    "lung cancer."
  ATTRIBUTION / FRAMING BIAS — attributing symptoms to a known condition or to
    the patient's demographic ("she's young", "he's a smoker, of course he
    coughs").
  CONTINUITY FAILURE — the patient sees a different provider each time (a
    quack/RMP, then a PHC MO, then a private clinic). Each provider restarts
    from zero. This is the dominant mechanism in rural India and is NOT a
    cognitive bias at all — it is an INFORMATION ARCHITECTURE failure.
  SAFETY-NETTING FAILURE — the clinician intends "come back if it doesn't
    settle" but it is never documented, never timed, and never followed up.

  AIRA's design answer, stated crisply:
    "We do not try to out-diagnose the doctor. We fix the two failures that are
     not clinical at all: the patient has no memory of their own care, and
     nobody sets a timer on 'come back if it doesn't settle.' We supply both."

C3. INDIA-SPECIFIC CONFOUNDERS (know these, they show local depth)

  - High TB prevalence -> persistent cough / weight loss / haemoptysis are
    anchored to TB. Anti-TB therapy is often given empirically for 2–6 months.
    A lung cancer can lose half a year to this.
  - Oral lesions are attributed to "ulcer from spicy food" or trauma from a
    sharp tooth; treated with topical agents repeatedly.
  - Post-coital / irregular bleeding attributed to "hormonal problem" or
    infection; cervical cancer commonly presents this way.
  - Breast lumps attributed to fibroadenoma or lactation-related change,
    especially in younger women (Indian breast cancer peaks ~a decade earlier
    than in Western populations).
  - A large share of first contact is with informal/unqualified providers
    (RMPs, pharmacists, traditional healers) who cannot investigate at all and
    leave no record.
  - Fear and fatalism: "cancer = death sentence" beliefs suppress help-seeking,
    which is exactly why your tone must be non-alarming. This is a design
    requirement, not politeness.

PART D — SCREENING SCIENCE
================================================================================

Most hackathon teams say "screening" without knowing what it means. If a
clinician judge is on the panel, this section is where you either win or lose.

D1. THE DISTINCTION EVERY TEAM MISSES

  WHO draws a sharp line between two different things:

  EARLY DIAGNOSIS  — applies to people WITH SYMPTOMS. The goal is to shorten
    the interval from symptom onset to diagnosis and treatment. Appropriate at
    ALL resource levels.

  SCREENING        — applies to people WITHOUT SYMPTOMS (asymptomatic target
    population). Testing an entire defined population to find disease before it
    declares itself. Only justified when a whole programme exists: recall,
    diagnosis, treatment, follow-up, quality assurance.

  AIRA DOES BOTH, AND YOU SHOULD SAY SO EXPLICITLY:
    - Persistence Clock + Loop Detector + Handoff Card = EARLY DIAGNOSIS arm
    - Screening Passport                               = SCREENING arm
  Making this distinction out loud immediately marks you as the team that read
  the literature.

D2. WILSON & JUNGNER CRITERIA (1968, WHO) — the canonical screening test

  A condition is worth screening for if, broadly:
    1. The condition is an important health problem
    2. There is an accepted treatment
    3. Facilities for diagnosis and treatment are available
    4. There is a recognisable latent or early symptomatic stage
    5. There is a suitable, acceptable test
    6. The natural history is adequately understood
    7. There is an agreed policy on whom to treat
    8. Case-finding is cost-balanced
    9. Case-finding is continuous, not a one-off project

  Point 3 and 9 are the ones India struggles with, and the ones your ASHA
  dashboard addresses.

D3. THE THREE BIASES YOU MUST BE ABLE TO DEFINE

  If a judge asks "how do you know early detection actually helps?" — this is
  the answer they're testing for.

  LEAD-TIME BIAS
    Detecting a cancer earlier automatically makes survival-from-diagnosis look
    longer, even if the person dies on exactly the same date. You have added
    "lead time," not life. This is why screening programmes are evaluated on
    MORTALITY, not on survival time.

  LENGTH-TIME BIAS
    Screening preferentially catches slow-growing tumours (they spend longer in
    the detectable pre-clinical phase), which have better prognosis anyway. This
    inflates apparent screening benefit.

  OVERDIAGNOSIS
    Detecting disease that would never have caused symptoms or death in the
    person's lifetime. Leads to overtreatment — real harm, no benefit. This is
    the strongest argument against indiscriminate screening.

  HOW AIRA ANSWERS THIS — memorise:
    "We do not invent screening recommendations. We route people into
     screening programmes that national and WHO guidance has already judged
     to be net-beneficial, at the ages and intervals those guidelines specify.
     Our contribution is COMPLIANCE and TIMING, not a new claim about benefit.
     That is deliberate — it is how we avoid overdiagnosis."

  That answer is bulletproof.

D4. TEST PERFORMANCE — AND WHY PPV COLLAPSES

  SENSITIVITY = TP/(TP+FN)  — of those with disease, how many we catch.
  SPECIFICITY = TN/(TN+FP)  — of those without, how many we correctly clear.
  PPV = TP/(TP+FP)          — of those we flag, how many actually have it.
  NPV = TN/(TN+FN)          — of those we clear, how many are truly clear.

  CRITICAL: sensitivity and specificity are properties of the TEST. PPV and NPV
  depend on PREVALENCE. At low prevalence, PPV collapses.

  Worked example — say it on stage, it is devastatingly effective:
    Test: 90% sensitivity, 95% specificity. Disease prevalence 0.5%.
    Population 100,000 -> 500 have cancer, 99,500 do not.
      True positives  = 0.90 x 500    = 450
      False positives = 0.05 x 99,500 = 4,975
      PPV = 450 / (450 + 4,975) = 8.3%
    A 95%-specific test still produces 11 false alarms for every true one.

  THE DESIGN CONSEQUENCE FOR AIRA — state this as a deliberate choice:
    "Because of this, AIRA is engineered for HIGH SENSITIVITY AND HIGH NPV, and
     it never outputs a diagnosis. Its positive output is 'this warrants
     evaluation' — an action that costs a clinic visit — not 'you have cancer.'
     We deliberately accept low PPV because the cost asymmetry demands it:
     a false positive costs one consultation; a false negative costs a stage."

D5. THE NICE 3% RULE — the single most useful number in this project

  NICE guideline NG12 ("Suspected cancer: recognition and referral", 2015,
  England) restructured cancer referral around an explicit RISK THRESHOLD:

    If a symptom/sign combination carries a >= 3% POSITIVE PREDICTIVE VALUE for
    cancer, that patient should enter an urgent (2-week-wait) suspected-cancer
    pathway or urgent direct-access investigation.

  Before 2015, thresholds were implicit and mostly above 5%. The guideline
  development group lowered it to 3% explicitly to catch more cancers earlier,
  accepting the cost. Exceptions exist (notably children/young people, where
  the threshold is lower still).

  NG12 also mandates SAFETY NETTING for patients who do NOT meet the threshold
  but have concerning symptoms — documented "come back if..." advice.

  WHY THIS MATTERS ENORMOUSLY TO YOU:
    1. It proves a real national health system already accepts an EXPLICIT,
       QUANTIFIED, LOW risk threshold for escalation. Your system is not
       inventing a philosophy — it is implementing an existing, published one.
    2. NG12 is FREE, PUBLIC, structured by symptom and age, and directly
       encodable as JSON rules. It is the best available source for your
       Persistence Clock safe-windows and referral triggers.
    3. "Safety netting" is literally the clinical name for the thing AIRA
       automates. USE THAT PHRASE. Say: "AIRA is automated, longitudinal,
       patient-held safety netting." A clinician judge will sit up.

  Note for honesty: NG12 is a UK guideline. For India, pair it with ICMR/NHM
  operational guidance (below) and say you use NG12 for symptom-level referral
  thresholds and Indian guidance for population screening — and that the
  ruleset is versioned and swappable per jurisdiction. That is a strength
  (configurable), not a weakness.

D6. THE INDIAN AND WHO SCREENING GUIDANCE YOU SHOULD ENCODE

  ICMR / NHM — Operational Framework: Management of Common Cancers, and the
  NP-NCD programme. Population-based screening targets ADULTS 30 YEARS AND
  ABOVE for three cancers:
      ORAL     — oral visual examination
      BREAST   — clinical breast examination (CBE)
      CERVICAL — visual inspection with acetic acid (VIA)
  Nominally at ~5-year intervals, delivered through Health & Wellness Centres.

  WHO cervical cancer elimination strategy — the "90-70-90" targets by 2030:
      90% of girls fully HPV-vaccinated by age 15
      70% of women screened with a high-performance test by 35 and again by 45
      90% of women with cervical disease receiving treatment
  WHO now recommends HPV DNA testing as the preferred primary screening test.

  USPSTF — useful as an international reference set (mammography, cervical
  cytology/HPV, colorectal from 45, lung CT for heavy smokers). Use as fallback
  and for the "configurable per jurisdiction" argument.

D7. THE STATISTIC THAT SHOULD BE ON A SLIDE BY ITSELF

  NFHS-5 (National Family Health Survey, 2019–21) — % of women who have EVER
  been screened:

        CERVICAL CANCER  ................  1.9%
        BREAST CANCER    ................  0.9%
        ORAL CANCER      ................  0.9%

  Best-performing states: Tamil Nadu (cervical 9.8%, breast 5.6%), Puducherry
  (7.4% / 4.2%), Mizoram (6.9% / 2.7%). Andaman & Nicobar highest for oral among
  women (10.1%). Poorer wealth quintiles screen significantly less.
  Source: J Family Med Prim Care, "Status of cancer screening in India: an alarm
  signal from NFHS-5", 2022/2023.

  THE LINE TO SAY:
    "India has a national cancer screening programme. Coverage is under two
     percent. The policy is not missing — the LAST MILE is missing. AIRA is
     built for the last mile."

  That reframes your project from "new idea" to "delivery layer for existing
  national policy," which is exactly what makes it adoptable by government.

PART F — THE AI/ML LANDSCAPE
================================================================================

F1. PRIOR ART YOU MUST KNOW (so you can say what's new)

  QCANCER (Hippisley-Cox & Coupland, QResearch / Univ. of Nottingham)
    - Risk algorithms estimating the absolute risk that a patient has a
      currently UNDIAGNOSED cancer, from symptoms + risk factors.
    - Derived and validated on UK QResearch primary-care records: 452 practices
      for derivation, 224 for validation. Separate algorithms for men and women
      (BJGP 2013).
    - Features: red-flag symptoms (weight loss, abdominal pain, indigestion,
      dysphagia, abnormal bleeding, lumps), general symptoms (fatigue,
      constipation), and risk factors (age, family history, smoking, alcohol,
      deprivation, BMI, comorbidities).
    - Used within the NHS to flag patients at high risk of undiagnosed cancer.

    THIS IS YOUR STRONGEST CREDIBILITY ANCHOR. It proves the core idea —
    symptom + risk-factor models predicting undiagnosed cancer — is validated,
    deployed national practice, not speculation.

    THEN STATE THE GAP HONESTLY:
      "QCancer is cross-sectional and requires a complete electronic GP record.
       It answers 'given this snapshot, what is the risk?' It cannot answer
       'this person has been treated twice and is not improving.' And it cannot
       run where there is no GP record at all. AIRA is longitudinal,
       patient-held, and works with no prior record and no network."

    That is a precise, defensible novelty claim. Much better than "we use AI."

  OTHER PRIOR ART WORTH ONE LINE EACH:
    - Risk models: Gail model (breast), Tyrer-Cuzick/IBIS (breast, incl. family
      history), PLCO_m2012 (lung). These estimate FUTURE risk in asymptomatic
      people — different task from QCancer's "currently undiagnosed."
      Know the distinction: RISK PREDICTION (future) vs CASE-FINDING (present).
    - Symptom checkers (Ada, Babylon, Isabel) — general triage, not
      cancer-specific, not longitudinal, repeatedly criticised for poor
      real-world accuracy and lack of validation. Do not compare yourself
      favourably to them without evidence; instead say your decisions are
      guideline-deterministic, unlike LLM symptom chat.
    - Oral lesion CNNs — a real, published literature on smartphone-based oral
      lesion classification for low-resource screening. Your camera module sits
      in an established research line.

F2. MODEL SELECTION — AND THE ARGUMENT FOR IT

  THE REQUIREMENT: the problem statement says "ethical and EXPLAINABLE."
  That word should drive your architecture, and you should say so.

  RECOMMENDED PRIMARY MODEL: EXPLAINABLE BOOSTING MACHINE (EBM)
    - A Generalised Additive Model with pairwise interactions (GA2M), from
      Microsoft Research's InterpretML.
    - Form:  g(E[y]) = b0 + SUM_i f_i(x_i) + SUM_ij f_ij(x_i, x_j)
    - It is a GLASS BOX: each feature's contribution is an exact, plottable
      function. Not a post-hoc approximation.
    - Accuracy is typically competitive with random forests / boosted trees on
      tabular clinical data.

    THE STAGE ARGUMENT (this is a genuinely strong technical position):
      "We deliberately did not use a black box with SHAP explanations bolted on.
       SHAP is an APPROXIMATION of a model you cannot inspect. In a clinical
       decision-support system, we wanted the explanation to BE the model. An
       Explainable Boosting Machine gives us an exact per-feature contribution
       curve — we can show a clinician the precise shape of how risk varies with
       age, and they can audit it. Interpretability here is a safety property,
       not a UI feature."

    If a judge pushes on accuracy: EBMs are competitive on tabular data, and in
    this domain calibration matters more than raw discrimination (see F4).

  CONTRAST WITH WHAT NOT TO SAY:
    Drop "transformer-based NLP for symptom classification" from the old deck.
    A transformer to map "mouth sore" to an ontology term is over-engineering
    that breaks offline and adds latency. Use a small multilingual sentence
    embedding model (e.g. a MiniLM/LaBSE-class encoder) running IN-BROWSER via
    onnxruntime-web for free-text -> symptom-ontology matching, and say exactly
    that. Right-sized engineering reads as maturity.

  THE ARCHITECTURAL PRINCIPLE TO STATE OUT LOUD:
      "The RULES make the decision. The MODEL ranks and prioritises. The LLM
       only phrases the result in the user's language. No generative model is
       ever in the decision path."
    This single sentence answers the ethics, explainability, safety AND
    hallucination questions simultaneously. It is the most valuable sentence in
    your architecture section.

F3. THE DATA PROBLEM — AND THE HONEST ANSWER

  There is NO large public labelled dataset of Indian primary-care symptom
  trajectories with cancer outcomes. Pretending otherwise will get you caught.

  The defensible approach — say it in these words:

  "We built an EPIDEMIOLOGICALLY-GROUNDED SYNTHETIC COHORT. Rather than
   inventing data, we encoded published conditional probabilities — symptom PPVs
   from Hamilton et al., age/sex incidence from GLOBOCAN and the NCRP, stage
   distributions from Indian registries, and consultation-count distributions
   from NHS audit data — into a generative simulator, then sampled trajectories
   from it. The model learns the epidemiology that the literature already
   established. We are explicit that this validates the PIPELINE, not clinical
   performance; clinical validation requires a prospective cohort, and we say so
   on the slide."

  That is a mature answer. Judges respect stated limitations far more than
  inflated accuracy claims.

  REAL DATA YOU CAN ACTUALLY USE:
    - SEER (US NCI) — public, registry-scale, has stage/site/age/survival. Great
      for the stage->survival impact model and for grounding your "stage shift"
      claim quantitatively.
    - NCRP / ICMR published reports — Indian incidence and stage priors.
    - NFHS-5 microdata — screening coverage and its socioeconomic gradient;
      useful for the dashboard's equity view.
    - Public oral lesion image sets (Mendeley "Oral Cancer (Lips and Tongue)
      images"; Kaggle/Roboflow OSCC and oral lesion collections) for the vision
      module. Report the exact size and class balance. Do not oversell.

F4. METRICS — GET THIS RIGHT OR LOSE THE ML JUDGE

  NEVER REPORT ACCURACY. With ~0.5% prevalence, a model that says "no cancer"
  every time is 99.5% accurate and completely useless. If you show an accuracy
  number, an ML judge will end you.

  REPORT THESE INSTEAD:
    - SENSITIVITY / RECALL at a fixed operating point — your primary metric.
    - SPECIFICITY and the resulting referral volume (workload cost).
    - PPV and NPV *at stated prevalence* — always state the prevalence.
    - AUROC — discrimination. Fine, but not sufficient alone.
    - AUPRC — far more informative than AUROC under heavy class imbalance.
    - CALIBRATION — this is the one that matters most clinically and the one
      teams forget:
        * Calibration curve / reliability diagram
        * Brier score
        * Expected Calibration Error (ECE)
      A model that says "12% risk" must be right about 12 times in 100.
      Discrimination without calibration is unusable for a threshold-based
      referral rule like NICE's 3%.
    - DECISION CURVE ANALYSIS (Vickers & Elkin) — plots NET BENEFIT against
      threshold probability. It is THE accepted way to show a clinical
      prediction model is actually useful, because it weighs true positives
      against false positives at clinically relevant thresholds. If you produce
      one decision curve, you will be the only team that did.

  ALSO REPORT: subgroup performance by age, sex, and urban/rural — this is your
  algorithmic-fairness evidence (see G4).

F5. THE THREE MODELS IN AIRA — KEEP THEM SEPARATE AND NAMED

    MODEL 1 — GENERAL CASE-FINDING MODEL (the core)
      Task:    multi-site cancer case-finding from age, sex, risk factors and
               current symptom set.
      Type:    Explainable Boosting Machine (glass box), calibrated.
      Output:  per-site risk + overall "warrants investigation" flag +
               exact per-feature contributions.
      Precedent: QCancer.

    MODEL 2 — TRAJECTORY / ESCALATION MODEL
      Task:    given a SEQUENCE of care episodes, score the concern that this
               trajectory is a delayed-diagnosis pathway.
      Type:    deterministic rules for the hard flags (auditable, guideline-
               cited) + a small sequence model (GRU or temporal CNN) producing
               a continuous "trajectory concern" score with per-episode
               attention weights as the explanation.
      Output:  Loop Level (L1/L2/L3) + reasoning trail.
      Novelty: THIS IS THE PART THAT DOES NOT EXIST IN PRIOR ART.

    MODEL 3 — ORAL LESION VISION MODEL (separate feature)
      Task:    triage an oral cavity image into a referral urgency band.
      Type:    MobileNetV3 / EfficientNet-Lite transfer learning, exported to
               ONNX (browser) and TFLite (Raspberry Pi).
      Output:  GREEN / AMBER / RED triage band + confidence + Grad-CAM overlay.
      Framing: TRIAGE, never diagnosis. A lesion is diagnosed by BIOPSY.
               Say that sentence if asked. It is the correct answer.

F6. CLASS IMBALANCE AND THRESHOLD HANDLING (be ready)

  Techniques to name if asked: class weighting, focal loss, stratified sampling,
  SMOTE (mention but note it can distort calibration — a sophisticated caveat),
  and — most importantly — THRESHOLD SELECTION driven by the cost asymmetry
  rather than by maximising F1. Tie the threshold to NICE's 3% PPV rule and you
  have a clinically justified operating point rather than an arbitrary one.
  That is a much better answer than "we used SMOTE."

PART G — ETHICS, FAILURE MODES, REGULATION
================================================================================

G1. THE ASYMMETRIC HARMS — KNOW WHICH IS WORSE

  FALSE POSITIVE: anxiety, cost of an unnecessary visit or test, occasionally
    an invasive procedure with real complication risk, and — in a poor household
    — lost wages and travel cost. Real harm. Not catastrophic.

  FALSE NEGATIVE: FALSE REASSURANCE. This is the danger that should keep you
    up at night, because AIRA could actively make things WORSE than no system.
    A person with a genuine red-flag symptom who is told "low risk" may delay
    longer than they otherwise would have.

  YOUR MITIGATIONS — have all four ready:
    1. HARD RED-FLAG OVERRIDE: a fixed list of symptoms (dysphagia,
       haemoptysis, post-menopausal bleeding, a lesion persisting past its safe
       window, a fixed breast lump with skin change, etc.) escalates
       IMMEDIATELY, bypassing any model score. The ML can only ESCALATE, never
       DE-ESCALATE below the rule floor.
    2. NO "ALL CLEAR" OUTPUT EVER. The lowest band is worded as "no action
       indicated right now — return if anything changes or persists," with a
       scheduled re-check date. Never "you are fine."
    3. SENSITIVITY-FIRST operating point, explicitly justified by cost
       asymmetry.
    4. Every screen carries the decision-support disclaimer.

  Saying "false reassurance is our most dangerous failure mode, and here is how
  we engineered against it" is a maturity signal almost no team will show.

G2. AUTOMATION BIAS

  Clinicians can over-trust an algorithm's output and under-weigh their own
  judgement — and the reverse, alert fatigue, where too many flags cause all
  flags to be ignored. Mitigations: show reasoning not just a score, require
  clinician override with a captured reason (which becomes training signal),
  and tune flag volume so the dashboard queue stays actionable. Say "we
  budget for a maximum flag rate per ASHA per week" — that is an operations
  answer, and it is the right one.

G3. THE DECISION-SUPPORT BOUNDARY (a hard constraint in the PS)

  Regulatory context, stated correctly:
    - SOFTWARE AS A MEDICAL DEVICE (SaMD) — the IMDRF framework classifies
      software by (a) the significance of the information it provides and
      (b) the seriousness of the condition.
    - In India, medical devices including certain software are regulated by
      CDSCO under the Medical Devices Rules 2017.
    - Software that INFORMS clinical management sits in a lower risk class than
      software that DRIVES or DIAGNOSES. AIRA is deliberately built as
      "informs" — it recommends a guideline-defined next step to a human, and a
      human decides.

  ENGINEERING THAT ENFORCES THE BOUNDARY (not just a disclaimer):
    - Output vocabulary is restricted to actions and risk tiers, never disease
      names as conclusions. "Warrants evaluation," "screening due,"
      "urgent referral indicated" — never "you likely have cancer."
    - No probability of malignancy is ever shown to a lay user.
    - Every recommendation displays its GUIDELINE CITATION, so the authority is
      the guideline, not the algorithm.
    - The Handoff Card is addressed to a CLINICIAN and frames AIRA as evidence,
      not verdict.
  Say: "the constraint is enforced in the output layer, not in a footer."

G4. ALGORITHMIC EQUITY

  Risks specific to this build:
    - Training on Western data (SEER, UK primary care) transfers imperfectly to
      Indian epidemiology — Indian breast cancer peaks roughly a decade earlier;
      oral cancer risk profile is dominated by smokeless tobacco rather than
      smoking; TB co-prevalence changes the meaning of respiratory symptoms.
      SAY THIS. It shows you know your data's provenance is a limitation.
    - Vision model trained largely on lighter-skinned or clinical-photography
      datasets may underperform on the actual field population and lighting.
      Mitigation: standardised illumination (your ESP32 LED ring), report
      subgroup performance, and state the limitation.
    - Digital access itself is unequal — the poorest have the worst access to
      the tool designed to help them. Mitigation: ASHA-mediated use and an
      IVR/SMS path, so the individual does not need a smartphone.

G5. PRIVACY

  DPDP Act 2023 principles: consent, purpose limitation, data minimisation,
  storage limitation. Design answers: on-device inference (raw data never has to
  leave), local encryption, explicit consent at onboarding with a
  voice-explained plain-language notice for low-literacy users, and the ability
  to use the app anonymously without ABHA linkage. Note: a low-literacy user
  cannot give meaningful consent from a wall of text — voice consent is an
  ETHICS feature, not just an accessibility one.

PART H — STAGE ARGUMENTS AND ANTICIPATED QUESTIONS
================================================================================

H1. THE FIVE SENTENCES TO MEMORISE VERBATIM

  1. "India's cancer mortality-to-incidence ratio is around 0.65 against roughly
      0.4 in high-income countries. That gap is not mainly about treatment. It
      is about stage at presentation."

  2. "Rectal bleeding predicts colorectal cancer about two times in a hundred.
      The doctor who says 'it's probably piles' is right 98 times out of 100.
      The error is not the first benign explanation — it is the second and third,
      when nobody updates on the fact that the benign explanation has failed."

  3. "In the NHS — universal coverage, electronic records, a national two-week
      referral standard — one in five cancer patients still saw their GP three
      or more times before referral. Now remove all of those safeguards. That is
      rural India."

  4. "India already has a national cancer screening programme for adults over
      thirty. NFHS-5 puts coverage at 1.9% for cervical and 0.9% for breast and
      oral. The policy is not missing. The last mile is."

  5. "CBAC is a one-time paper snapshot. AIRA is CBAC made longitudinal,
      digital and self-escalating. We are not asking India to adopt a new
      instrument — we are giving an instrument it already mandates a memory."

H2. LIKELY JUDGE QUESTIONS AND YOUR ANSWERS

  Q: "Isn't this just another symptom checker?"
  A: "No. A symptom checker is cross-sectional — it asks what your symptoms mean
      today. Our core novelty is the second-order signal: what does it mean that
      this symptom has now SURVIVED TWO FAILED TREATMENTS and passed its
      guideline safe window? No deployed system computes that, because no system
      holds the longitudinal patient-held record. Also, our decisions are
      deterministic and guideline-cited, not generated."

  Q: "How do you know your model is any good with no real data?"
  A: "We don't claim clinical performance and we say so on the slide. We built an
      epidemiologically-grounded synthetic cohort by encoding published
      conditional probabilities — Hamilton's symptom PPVs, GLOBOCAN and NCRP
      incidence, Indian stage distributions, NHS consultation-count
      distributions — and sampled from it. That validates the pipeline and the
      logic. Clinical validation requires a prospective cohort with registry
      linkage, which is our stated next step. What IS validated today is the
      rule layer, because it is a direct encoding of NICE NG12 and ICMR
      operational guidance."

  Q: "What if you're wrong and someone doesn't go to a doctor because of you?"
  A: "False reassurance is our most dangerous failure mode and we engineered
      against it specifically. Three things: we never output an all-clear — the
      lowest band is 'no action indicated now, return if anything changes,' with
      a scheduled re-check. A hard-coded red-flag list escalates immediately and
      the ML cannot de-escalate below that rule floor. And we run at a
      deliberately sensitivity-first operating point because a false positive
      costs one consultation while a false negative costs a stage."

  Q: "Aren't you going to cause mass panic and overload the PHCs?"
  A: "Two answers. On tone — we never show a cancer probability to a lay user;
      the output is an action and a timeframe, and the language is calm and
      specific. On load — we do not lower the referral bar, we implement NICE's
      published 3% PPV threshold, the same bar the NHS already accepts. And the
      dashboard is a PRIORITISED QUEUE with a flag-rate budget, so a health
      worker gets a workable number of actions, not an undifferentiated alert
      list."

  Q: "Isn't more screening always good?"
  A: "No, and that's important. Screening carries overdiagnosis — detecting
      disease that would never have harmed the person — plus lead-time and
      length-time bias, which make screening look better than it is. That is
      exactly why we do NOT invent screening recommendations. We route people
      into programmes that WHO and ICMR have already judged net-beneficial, at
      the ages and intervals they specify. Our contribution is compliance and
      timing, not a new claim about benefit."

  Q: "Why not just use a large language model for all of it?"
  A: "Because a generative model in the decision path is unauditable and can
      hallucinate a clinical recommendation. Our architecture is explicit: the
      RULES make the decision, the model ranks and prioritises, and the LLM only
      phrases the already-made decision in the user's language. That is also what
      lets the whole decision path run offline on a phone."

  Q: "Why an EBM instead of XGBoost with SHAP?"
  A: "SHAP is a post-hoc approximation of a model you cannot inspect. An
      Explainable Boosting Machine is a glass-box GAM — each feature's
      contribution is an exact plottable function a clinician can audit. On
      tabular clinical data the accuracy is competitive, and in this domain
      calibration matters more than raw discrimination anyway, because our
      referral rule is a probability threshold."

  Q: "What's your business/deployment model?"
  A: "Public health, not consumer. The deployment unit is a Health & Wellness
      Centre: the ASHA replaces her paper CBAC with AIRA, the CHO gets the
      prioritised queue, and the district gets coverage and stage-shift
      analytics. Interoperable with ABDM/ABHA and the existing CPHC-NCD
      application. Cost per screened adult is the metric a state health
      department would actually evaluate us on."

  Q: "How is this different from QCancer?"
  A: "QCancer is the closest prior art and it validates our premise — it's
      deployed in the NHS. But it is cross-sectional and requires a complete
      electronic GP record. It answers 'given this snapshot, what is the risk.'
      It cannot answer 'this person has been treated twice and is not
      improving,' and it cannot run where there is no GP record and no network.
      We are longitudinal, patient-held, and offline."

  Q: "What does the hardware actually add?"
  A: "Three specific things, not decoration. The Pi is an offline server so a
      village with no internet still gets the full application over local
      Wi-Fi. The IMU standardises capture — it blocks the shutter on motion blur
      and guides the operator to fixed view angles, so an untrained worker
      produces consistent images, which is the single biggest cause of field
      vision-model failure. The ESP32 fixes the illumination colour temperature,
      because inconsistent lighting is the second biggest cause."

H3. THE THINGS THAT WILL LOSE YOU THE ROOM — DON'T DO THEM

  - Reporting accuracy on an imbalanced problem.
  - Saying "our AI detects cancer." Say "flags for evaluation."
  - Claiming a clinical validation you did not do.
  - Using "transformer" or "deep learning" as a selling point with no reason.
  - Conflating screening (asymptomatic) with early diagnosis (symptomatic).
  - Showing a cancer probability to a simulated patient in the demo.
  - Blaming doctors. Blame the information architecture.
